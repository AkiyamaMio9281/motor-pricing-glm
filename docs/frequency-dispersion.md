Read against 1, the two standard estimators contradict each other: one says the

Read against the simulation, all three agree that the Poisson variance does not
hold. They disagree only about how much, and the next section is why.

## Where the Pearson statistic comes from

| Largest contributions | Share of rows | Share of the Pearson sum |
|---|---|---|
| 68 rows | 0.01% | 19.0% |
| 678 rows | 0.1% | 43.6% |
| 6,780 rows | 1% | 76.1% |

The top 0.1% of rows carry 43.6% of the statistic. Their median exposure is 0.020 of a year, 44% are flagged as short exposure, their median fitted claim count is 0.00215, and 542 of them had one claim and 136 had more. A claim where the model expected a few thousandths of one adds hundreds to the sum by itself. This is the exposure misfit D2-2 measured, showing up as variance. Without those rows the Pearson dispersion is 1.340.

The ratio of summed squared residuals to summed fitted claims weights each row by its fitted mean instead, so a handful of tiny-mean rows cannot dominate it. It reads 1.070: across the bulk of the book, variance exceeds the Poisson mean by about 7%.

## What it does to standard errors

| Standard errors | Median against Poisson | Largest against Poisson | Significant at 5% |
|---|---|---|---|
| Poisson | 1.000 | 1.000 | 51 of 76 |
| quasi-Poisson, scaled by the Pearson dispersion | 1.541 | 1.541 | 43 of 76 |
| sandwich, rows independent | 1.023 | 1.172 | 51 of 76 |
| sandwich, clustered by risk group | 1.084 | 1.258 | 50 of 76 |

Quasi-Poisson multiplies every standard error by 1.541, the square root of the Pearson dispersion, and would take 8 coefficients out of significance. The sandwich estimate, which assumes no variance function and is not dominated by tiny-mean rows, puts the understatement at 2.3% in the median with rows independent and 8.4% once pieces of the same policy are allowed to be correlated.

## Are the fragments behind the non-proportionality?

D2-3 raised the possibility that pieces of one policy-year recorded as separate short rows produce the non-proportionality D2-2 found. Reassembling each risk group into one row, 678,013 rows become 581,535 groups, 85,317 of them made of two or more pieces. Only 102 reassembled groups exceed one year of exposure, which is what pieces of a single policy-year would do.

| Estimated coefficient on log(exposure) | Coefficient | Std. error |
|---|---|---|
| policy-years as recorded | 0.411 | 0.0063 |
| risk groups reassembled | 0.481 | 0.0075 |

Reassembly moves the coefficient towards 1 and closes 12% of the distance. The fragmentation accounts for part of the non-proportionality, and most of it remains.

## Not done

No negative binomial model was fitted. It would replace the Poisson variance with mu + alpha mu^2 and so reweight the rows, moving the point estimates. The evidence above is that the variance problem is concentrated in a small set of short-exposure rows, with about 7% excess variance across the rest of the book. The sandwich estimator gives valid standard errors for the Poisson estimates without committing to a variance function, which is the question that needed answering.

## Conclusion

- The Poisson point estimates stand. Overdispersion on its own does not bias them;
  what bias they carry comes from the exposure relationship documented in D2-2, not
  from the variance.
- Standard errors and confidence intervals for the frequency model use
  `frequency_vcov(fit, cluster = risk_groups(frame))`.
- Quasi-Poisson rescaling is not used. It would read a misfit concentrated in a small fraction of rows as uncertainty in every coefficient, widening every standard error by 54% where the clustered sandwich finds 8% in the median.
