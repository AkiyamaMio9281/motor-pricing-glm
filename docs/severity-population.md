## Every way a zero can reach a Gamma fit

A Gamma model cannot take a zero. Whether a policy without an amount makes the fit
fail or disappears from it depends on how its missing amount was written.

| Response built from | Rows given | Outcome | Rows used | Rows silently dropped |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| all policies; total amount; unpriced as 0 | 678,013 | fails: non-positive values not allowed for the 'Gamma' family |  |  |
| all policies; total amount; unpriced left missing | 678,013 | runs | 24,944 | 653,069 |
| all policies; amount per claim; unpriced as 0 | 678,013 | fails: non-positive values not allowed for the 'Gamma' family |  |  |
| all policies; amount per claim; unpriced left missing | 678,013 | runs | 24,944 | 653,069 |
| claim_nb > 0; amount per claim; unpriced as 0 | 34,060 | fails: non-positive values not allowed for the 'Gamma' family |  |  |
| claim_nb > 0; amount per claim; unpriced left missing | 34,060 | runs | 24,944 | 9,116 |

Written as 0, every version fails. Left missing, every version runs, because `glm()` removes rows with a missing response before fitting and reports nothing. The silent versions land on 24,944 policies, the right population, by accident. What they never report is that restricting to `claim_nb > 0`, the usual advice, still handed the fit 9,116 policies whose reported claims have no amount.

`fit_severity()` refuses both cases by name instead of relying on either behaviour:

- missing amount: *1 claim has no amount. Severity is fitted on priced claims only; build the frame from model.severity_frame rather than from policies.*
- zero amount: *1 claim has a non-positive amount; a Gamma model cannot take it*

## Does frequency times severity reproduce recorded losses?

| Pure premium per policy-year | Value | Against recorded |
|---|---|---|
| recorded losses over exposure | 167.18 |  |
| frequency of reported claims times mean severity | 228.23 | 36.5% high |
| frequency of priced claims times mean severity | 167.18 | 0.0% |

The last row is an identity, not a finding: priced claims times their mean amount is recorded losses by definition, and it is there to anchor the comparison. The 36.5% is simply the ratio of reported claims, 36,102, to priced claims, 26,444.

Whether it is an overstatement depends on what an unpriced claim is, and this data cannot say. If unpriced claims cost nothing, closed without payment, then a frequency model of reported claims overprices the book by that margin. If they are claims whose amounts are missing from the file, recorded losses understate the true cost instead. 9,116 policies report claims with no amount at all, 1 has some claims priced and some not, and no policy has more priced claims than it reported.

By region, the share of claim-reporting policies with an unpriced claim runs from 16% in R82 to 53% in R21.

They are not a random subset. Unpriced claims sit on newer vehicles, shorter
exposures and particular regions, so no single scaling factor applied to the
frequency model can correct for them. Whichever claim count frequency is fitted on
changes relativities, not only the level, and that is what makes the choice matter
even though the data cannot settle it.

## Can a holdout choose the severity terms yet?

A rule was fixed before fitting: on the risk-group holdout used for frequency, the
frequency terms against a constant, lower Gamma deviance wins. It picked the
constant, by 194.0. One claim decides that.

| Fold-0 holdout, rated minus constant deviance | Value |
|---|---|
| all 5,311 claims | 194.0 |
| the single claim contributing most, an amount of 390,742 | 186.1 |
| without that claim | 7.9 |
| without the ten largest claims | -83.7 |
| claims at or below the median amount | -533.9 |
| claims above the 99th percentile | 435.1 |

| Holdout fold | Claims | Constant | Rated | Rated minus constant |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 5,311 | 9,064.4 | 9,258.3 | 194.0 |
| 1 | 5,274 | 8,954.1 | 8,849.0 | -105.1 |
| 2 | 5,412 | 11,549.8 | 9,587.1 | -1,962.7 |
| 3 | 5,254 | 8,536.5 | 8,994.7 | 458.2 |
| 4 | 5,193 | 8,514.7 | 7,636.8 | -878.0 |

The rated model predicts the bulk of claims better and a handful of very large ones worse, and which of those wins a fold depends on which large claims land in it. Across the five folds the difference changes sign, from -1,962.7 to 458.2. On amounts this heavy-tailed, a single holdout cannot choose between these specifications, so the rule's verdict is not taken.

The specification is left to D2-6, which treats large losses first. Disclosed so
that its choice is not presented as blind: while writing this analysis, the same
single-fold comparison was also run with claims capped at their 99.5th percentile,
and the rated model came out slightly ahead.
